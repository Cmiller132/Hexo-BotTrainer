# Inference-backend benchmarks — dense_cnn 96×6 + P7 (Model 1)

**Goal.** Decide what to adopt for the MCTS evaluator forward. Self-play is
GPU-forward-adjacent; the question is whether BF16, `torch.compile`, or TensorRT
beat the current FP16/AMP forward enough to matter end-to-end.

**Model.** `runs/dense_cnn_model1_target_96x6/checkpoints/bootstrap_sealbot_prefit.pt`
(96ch × 6 blocks, fully-conv P7 head, 2.122 M params, epoch 0 prefit), built from
`configs/dense_cnn_model1_target_96x6.toml`, `model_state` loaded **strict**, run
through `optimized_model1_for_inference` (conv/BN folded) + `channels_last` —
i.e. the *exact* production inference model.

**Hardware / SW.** RTX 4070 Ti (Ada sm_89, 60 SM), driver 595.71.
Native Windows: torch 2.10.0+cu126, cuDNN 9.10. WSL2 Ubuntu-24.04: torch
2.11.0+cu128. GPU confirmed free before runs (0% util, VRAM released); dashboard
left up on :8080.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

End-to-end (search-only, **estimated** via the 70%-forward attribution): FP16
58.8 pos/s → compile ~73 → TRT ~100. bs256 numbers and the full per-shape sweep
are in the detailed tables below; bs1 (single-game eval) is overhead/clock-bound
and covered separately. Backend wins do **not** stack with each other (pick one);
they **do** stack with the padding-waste fix.

## Definitions (so the table is unambiguous)
- **forward / "forwards/s"** = one network evaluation of one position (one MCTS
  leaf). `forwards/s = batch_rows / batch_latency`. This is the user's "~1287
  forwards/s" unit (a leaf eval = one position through the net).
- **bs** = forward batch size. The evaluator pads batches to power-of-two buckets.

## Methodology (rigor — these are the traps we got burned by before)
- **Representative inputs:** 2048 real positions generated by driving random-legal
  engine games to varied depths and encoding them through the **same Rust path the
  evaluator uses** (`rust_bridge.model1_batch_inputs`), nonzero-fraction ≈ 0.29.
  Correctness slice = a fixed 1024-row subset. Batches cycle through the pool (no
  single repeated row → no input-reuse caching artifact).
- **Warmup:** every variant warms up ≥ 6 s wall **and** ≥ 40 iters before timing,
  so the GPU clock ramps from idle (210 MHz) to boost (~2790 MHz) and cuDNN
  autotune converges. Clocks logged per run (idle→warm) to prove this.
- **Timing:** 300 CUDA-event-timed iterations; report mean, stdev, p50, p95.
- **Correctness gate:** every variant's policy logits AND value logits compared to
  the **FP32 reference** (same optimized model, no autocast) on the 1024-row slice.
  Report max-abs error on logits, on the *decoded scalar value*, and on the
  softmax policy. A fast-but-wrong forward silently corrupts training.
- **Production batch shape:** measured, not assumed — see "Real batch size" below.

## Real self-play attribution (this reframes the whole question)
Instrumented the **actual** production self-play loop (Rust MCTS, 512 visits,
256 active games, virtual_batch=4) over 2048 positions with the FP16 evaluator:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| warm self-play throughput (search-only loop) | **58.8 pos/s** |
| evaluator-callback share of search wall time | **78%** |
| Rust encode share | 10% · other MCTS (select/backup/apply) | 12% |
| **real MCTS leaf batch size** | **mean ≈ 99, p50 70, p95 228, max 245** |
| eval-cache hit rate | 76% (only ~49 unique net evals / position) |
| callback throughput at real batches | **3709 fwd/s** |

Two consequences:
1. **The real forward runs at bs ≈ 70–245 → power-of-two bucket 128/256, never
   1024.** So the production-relevant shapes are **bs128 (most common) and bs256**,
   and that is where every variant must be judged — bs1024 is a near-dead bucket
   in this config.
2. The 3709 fwd/s the callback actually achieves (vs the 7023 fwd/s raw bs128
   forward) is lost mostly to **padding waste** (70→128 = 55% useful; 99→128 = 77%)
   and the small-batch mix — **not** the forward dtype. (The ~12.8 pos/s in the
   live calibration was the cold-cuDNN / cold-clock artifact, not steady state.)

## Results — forward throughput (forwards/s; higher = better)

Native Windows (torch 2.10), production-relevant shapes in **bold**:

| dtype | bs1 | **bs128** | **bs256** | bs1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 (reference) | 651 | 2126 | 2513 | 2548 |
| **FP16/AMP (production baseline)** | 437* | **7023** | **6432** | 6443 |
| BF16/AMP | 532 | 6692 | 6175 | 6182 |

`*` bs1 FP16 number is depressed by a GPU downclock under the trivial load
(clock fell to ~1455 MHz mid-measurement); bs1 is the single-game-eval case, not
self-play. FP16 bs1024 = 6443 fwd/s reproduced across 3 independent processes
(<0.1% spread) — headline verified a second way.

WSL2 (torch 2.11) — eager + `torch.compile`:

| variant | bs1 | **bs128** | **bs256** | bs1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WSL eager FP16 | 654 | 7039 | 6446 | 6070 |
| **WSL torch.compile FP16 (max-autotune)** | **4805** (0.21 ms) | **9540** | **9257** | 8574 |
| compile speedup vs native FP16 | (eval latency 7×↓) | **1.36×** | **1.44×** | 1.33× |

TensorRT 11.0 (WSL, strongly-typed engine, opt profile bs1–1024):

| variant | bs1 | **bs128** | **bs256** | bs1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TRT FP16 | 2696 | **16770** | **17115** | 17338 |

## Per-variant findings

### BF16/AMP — effort: trivial (one-line dtype change). NOT adopted.
~5% **slower** than FP16 at every batch (Ada shares FP16/BF16 tensor-core
throughput, so BF16's only effect here is overhead) **and** ~9× worse numerically
(decoded-value max-err 0.079 vs FP16's 0.009). No upside; do not adopt.

### torch.compile (WSL, max-autotune) — effort: moderate. Strong forward win.
**1.33–1.44× over native FP16 at the production batches** (bs128 7023→9540,
bs256 6432→9257) and **correctness PASSES** (policy max-err 0.013, on par with
plain FP16). Compile wall ≈ 5 s/shape (~22 s for all four buckets), once per
process. At bs1 the cudagraphs in max-autotune cut single-eval latency from
~1.5 ms to **0.21 ms (7×)** — a real lever for single-game eval.
- **WSL itself is not the win:** WSL eager FP16 ≈ native FP16 at bs128 (7039 vs
  7023). The gain is the compiler (Triton/Inductor kernel fusion + cudagraphs).
- **Integration risk (significant):** the production evaluator runs on **native
  Windows torch 2.10**, where `torch.compile`/Inductor + Triton is poorly
  supported (this is *why* compile was tested in WSL). Adopting it means either
  (a) moving self-play to WSL, or (b) getting Inductor+Triton working on Windows.
  Also: cudagraphs needs static input buffers; the evaluator's bucket padding
  already gives static shapes (128/256), but the H2D copy must target the
  captured buffer — torch.compile handles this, but it must be validated under
  the real Rust callback.

### TensorRT 11.0 FP16 (WSL, strongly-typed) — effort: high. Biggest forward win.
**2.39× (bs128) / 2.66× (bs256) over native FP16**, flat ~16.8–17.3k fwd/s across
all batches (TRT saturates the GPU even at bs128). **Correctness PASSES**:
decoded-value max-err 0.011 (≈ FP16's 0.009), policy-softmax max 0.0086. Effort
was real: TRT 11.0 has **no FP16/BF16 builder flag** — had to discover it needs
*strongly-typed* networks (precision baked into the ONNX), export an FP16 ONNX,
and build with an optimization profile. Engine build ≈ 44 s (first), ≈ 5 s with a
warm timing cache. TRT **BF16** is slower (10.4k) and BF16-class inaccurate — skip.
- **Integration risk (highest):** TRT engines are TRT-version- and
  platform-specific — an engine built in WSL/TRT-11 will not load in native
  Windows; production would need TRT on Windows (Python 3.14 wheel availability
  unverified) **or** self-play moved to WSL. The engine must be **rebuilt or
  refit every epoch** (weights change each training round) — ~44 s build (or use
  the `REFIT` flag) amortized over a multi-minute self-play epoch is acceptable.
  Wiring requires routing the Rust MCTS callback through the TRT runtime instead
  of the PyTorch forward (the `evaluate_model1_payload` boundary).

## End-to-end attribution — what a faster forward actually buys

From the instrumented self-play probe + the small-batch callback attribution
(both **measured**):
- evaluator callback = **78%** of search wall; encode 10%; other MCTS 12%.
- at the production buckets (128/256) the callback is **~90% forward-compute**,
  ~10% fixed marshalling (frombuffer / H2D / legal-flat gather+softmax / D2H /
  tobytes — none of which a faster forward backend speeds up).
- ⇒ **~70% of search wall is speedup-able forward-compute**; ~30% (marshalling
  8% + encode 10% + other 12%) is not.

**Estimated** end-to-end search throughput (Amdahl, speedup applied to the 70%),
from the measured warm baseline of **58.8 search-pos/s**:

| backend | forward speedup (measured, bs128–256) | search speedup (est.) | search pos/s (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| native FP16 (baseline) | 1.0× | 1.0× | 58.8 |
| torch.compile FP16 | ~1.4× | ~1.25× | ~73 |
| TensorRT FP16 | ~2.4× | ~1.7× | ~100 |

Caveats (why these are *estimates*, not measurements): (a) the full **training**
self-play also does sample finalization + NPZ shard writing, which my probe
omits — so the per-epoch wall speedup is *smaller* than the search-only speedup
above; (b) I could not measure end-to-end with the optimized backend wired in,
because the Rust MCTS extension isn't built for WSL and the optimized backends
don't run on native Windows — the exact integration barrier this report flags.

### A separate, higher-ROI, zero-risk lever: padding waste
The real MCTS leaf batch is mean ≈ 99 / **p50 70**, but the evaluator pads to the
power-of-two bucket **128**. So **~23–45% of every forward's rows are zeros**.
This is why the callback delivers only 3709 useful fwd/s vs the 7023 raw bs128
rate. Tighter buckets (e.g. 64/96/128 instead of pure powers of two — TRT/compile
static shapes don't need powers of two) would recover much of that **with no
dtype change and no correctness risk** — orthogonal to, and stackable with, any
backend choice.

## Headline verification (second method)
Every headline was re-measured in a **fresh process** with an **independent
wall-clock timer** alongside CUDA events; the two methods agree to **< 0.31%** and
reproduce the originals: native FP16 bs128 7045/7023 (orig 7039); compile bs128
9523/9511 (orig 9540); TRT bs128 16839/16814 (orig 16770); TRT bs256 17387/17375
(orig 17115). Native FP16 bs1024 (6443) also reproduced across 3 separate runs.

## Recommendation

1. **Keep FP16/AMP as the default.** It is correct (decoded-value err 0.009), 2.5×
   over FP32, and already shipped. **Reject BF16** outright — slower than FP16 on
   Ada *and* ~9× less accurate, in both autocast and TRT form.
2. **Do the padding-waste fix first** (bucketing). It is measured, zero-risk, needs
   no new runtime, and is worth a meaningful chunk on its own. Highest ROI per
   unit effort.
3. **Adopt TensorRT FP16 if you want the maximum forward win** (2.4–2.7× forward,
   est. ~1.7× search throughput → ~100 search-pos/s) **and** are willing to pay the
   integration cost: TRT on the production OS (or move self-play to WSL) + per-epoch
   engine rebuild/refit + routing the Rust callback through the TRT runtime.
   Correctness is acceptable as-is, but **gate any adoption on a SealBot-strength
   check** (decoded-value err 0.011 is tiny but compounds over 512-sim search).
4. **torch.compile is the middle option** (1.3–1.4× forward, est. ~1.25× search;
   plus a 7× single-eval latency drop from cudagraphs) at lower integration
   effort *if self-play runs in WSL* — but it does **not** run on the production
   native-Windows torch 2.10 today, so on Windows it is currently a non-starter.

**Suggested order:** (1) padding/bucketing fix → (2) re-measure → (3) if more is
needed, TensorRT FP16 in a WSL self-play path (which also unlocks torch.compile).
Net realistic target with TRT FP16 + bucketing: **search throughput ~1.7–2× over
today**, contingent on the integration work and a strength re-validation.
